When a small share is enough

In fixed-budget world-model training, a small share of specialist footage can already lift object persistence, while larger allocations mainly lower one-step prediction error.

Setting the mixture requires separating coverage, retention, and opportunity cost from single-step loss.

Sicheng Hu

Introduction

Training a world model under a fixed compute or data budget requires combining broad scene distributions with specialist interaction footage. Increasing a specialist source’s mixture weight reliably lowers prediction error on held-out clips from that source, but physical reasoning in closed-loop rollouts does not necessarily scale with it. World-model training therefore faces a core data-composition problem: how much specialist data earns its share of a fixed update budget when single-step loss and rollout capability diverge.

Occlusion makes this divergence concrete. Consider a ball rolling behind a screen: while the ball is hidden, predicting an empty tabletop minimizes single-frame error even if the model has lost track of the object. Whether the model preserves object identity only becomes observable several steps later, when the same ball must re-emerge on the opposite side. In this post, we distinguish two evaluation regimes that respond differently to data composition:

  • One-step prediction error. Measures how accurately the model predicts the next observation conditioned on ground-truth context from a held-out source. Expanding a specialist source’s share increases direct gradient exposure to that distribution and typically drives this error down.
  • Closed-loop rollout capability. Measures whether the model preserves physical invariants—such as contact interactions and object persistence—when rolling forward over multiple steps. Sustaining this capability requires exposing the model to the target transitions without eroding the general scene dynamics learned from broad data.

This separation appears directly in controlled video-model comparisons initialized from the same weights under a matched training budget. Relative to the base checkpoint, the smallest tested allocation of an interaction-rich source already improved both interaction and object-persistence scores. Assigning a larger allocation continued to lower prediction error on fresh clips from that source, whereas the two rollout scores remained largely level beyond their initial gains (Figure 1).

Under these dynamics, a small specialist allocation is justified because it supplies direct evidence of the target physical behavior; raising that allocation further requires an additional failure mode that extra exposure can actually resolve, rather than lower one-step error on familiar clips. In this post, we analyze why a small share is often sufficient and organize the trade-off around three mechanisms:

  • Event coverage. Under a finite budget, encounter probability follows diminishing returns: common transitions saturate after a few clips while rarer multi-object interactions remain under-covered.
  • Replay and retention. Public continual-pretraining studies show that a small replay share recovers most prior-domain performance without stalling adaptation, turning mixture selection into an explicit retention target.
  • Gradient opportunity cost. Because every specialist update displaces a general-domain update, the net change on a target loss depends on the difference in gradient alignment between the added and displaced sources.

Event coverage

Under a fixed data budget, the first few specialist clips produce the steepest increase in the probability of encountering a target physical transition, whereas subsequent slots yield sharply diminishing coverage gains.

Consider an analytical toy model with a fixed budget of 36 clips, partitioned between specialist footage and general-domain scenes. Assume that only specialist clips contain the target events: a simple single-object crossing occurs in one out of four specialist clips on average (r = 1/4), while a rarer two-object crossing occurs in one out of sixteen (r = 1/16).

Allocate s slots to the specialist source. If each independent draw contains a given transition with probability r, the probability of missing it across all s draws is (1 − r)s. The probability of encountering the event at least once in the budget is therefore

C(s)=1−(1−r)s

At s = 8 specialist clips, the simple crossing already reaches a 90.0% encounter probability, and adding a ninth slot raises that probability by only 2.5 percentage points (Figure 2). For the rarer two-object crossing, encounter probability stands at 40.3%, and the same ninth slot adds approximately 3.7 percentage points. A single additional slot therefore carries different marginal value across behaviors. Note that C(s) captures exposure opportunity rather than guaranteed mastery; whether the model converts exposure into reliable dynamics must still be verified in rollout.

This distinction also separates data collection from data reweighting. Upsampling a familiar clip grants the model another gradient update on an already-covered transition, whereas adding a recording of an unrepresented interaction supplies the model’s first exposure to that regime. Run logs should consequently report both the count of distinct recordings per source and the fraction of optimizer updates those recordings receive.

Event coverage explains why the earliest specialist slots yield the steepest gains when acquiring a target behavior. A parallel small-share effect governs the complementary regime: preserving an existing capability while continuing training on a new distribution.

Replay and retention

In continual pretraining, retaining a small replay share from the original distribution recovers most of the forgotten performance while leaving adaptation on the new distribution intact.

Public continual-pretraining benchmarks quantify this effect directly. Ibrahim et al. continued training a 10B-parameter language model originally trained on the Pile using SlimPajama under a matched token budget, comparing 0% replay against a 5% replay share drawn from the old source [1] (Table 1).

Table 1 Final validation loss in the 10B continual-pretraining replay comparison (Ibrahim et al., Table 4 [1]).
Old-source share Old-source loss New-source loss
0% 1.98 2.00
5% 1.79 2.00

Before the distribution switch, old-source validation loss stood at 1.75. Without replay, old-source loss rose to 1.98; mixing in 5% old-source tokens brought it back to 1.79—recovering roughly four-fifths of the increase—while new-source loss remained unchanged at 2.00 to the reported precision. Once a retention threshold is specified, “enough” ceases to be qualitative and becomes a concrete target on the retention–adaptation trade-off.

Ye et al. turn this relationship into a predictive selection rule through empirical data mixing laws, fitting validation loss as a function of mixture weights [2]. When continuing a language model on Python code alongside its original text distribution, they use the fitted curves to predict the minimum original-text share required to maintain baseline text loss. Specifying the retention target directly determines the operating point on the curve.

Replay experiments establish that a small share can satisfy a defined retention target on its own domain. Under a fixed training budget, however, evaluating a mixture requires accounting not only for what the added source contributes, but also for what the displaced source forfeits.

Gradient opportunity cost

Under a fixed update budget, raising one source’s mixture weight displaces optimizer steps from another, so the net effect on a target loss depends on the difference between the two sources’ gradient alignment rather than on the specialist source alone.

Evaluating that trade-off first requires an unambiguous unit for mixture shares. Suppose a long-video batch fits two clips while a short-video batch fits six. Alternating between those batches gives long videos 50% of optimizer updates and 25% of sampled clips, while raw file inventory yields a third number. For a trainer that draws one source per step, the relevant accounting unit is the fraction of optimizer updates each source actually receives.

Now consider a single small gradient step with learning rate η. Let gS denote the mean gradient from specialist data, gB the mean gradient from the broad-domain data giving up share, and gT the gradient of a target evaluation loss. Shifting a sampling-probability mass δ from broad to specialist data changes the target loss, to first order relative to the original update, by

ΔLT≈−ηδ⟨gT,gS−gB⟩

The inner product measures how strongly each update aligns with a descent direction for the target loss. Even a specialist update with positive alignment (⟨gT, gS⟩ > 0) is a net loss if the displaced broad-domain update aligned more strongly. Fan et al. use gradient alignment with target domains to learn domain sampling weights in DoGE [3]; writing the reallocation explicitly as gS − gB makes the opportunity cost of a specific swap transparent.

In video world-model runs, this indirect cost shows up across domains that were never touched directly. Certain collections of general real-world scenes transfer positively to other real scenes, reducing prediction error outside their own subset. Specialist interaction footage exhibits much weaker transfer to those broad collections. Reallocating updates away from a broadly transferable source can therefore degrade a third domain whose nominal mixture weight never changed.

First-order gradient alignment explains why over-allocating specialist data can erode general scene dynamics. Even when two mixtures yield nearly identical single-step losses, however, their predictions can still separate once states are fed back into the model over multi-step rollouts.

Rollout compounding

Because autoregressive world models condition each step on their own prior outputs, small errors compound across time—making multi-step rollout evaluation essential for separating mixtures that look equivalent under one-step loss.

In a separate video comparison, single-step prediction error barely separated two candidate mixtures, and short-horizon memory probes also produced closely matched scores. The gap emerged only when the model had to carry scene state across longer sequences. Once an autoregressive model generates an inaccurate state, that state becomes the conditioning input for its next prediction, causing errors to accumulate over the trajectory. Talvitie gives an explicit construction showing how two dynamics models with identical one-step prediction errors can behave very differently when rolled forward [4].

Determining whether a larger specialist share earns its update budget therefore calls for a controlled comparison protocol rather than a single-step loss sweep:

  • Matched initialization and fixed update budget. Start all candidate runs from the same checkpoint, hold the total number of optimizer updates constant, and record explicitly which source yields updates when specialist share increases.
  • Paired short- and long-horizon rollout scoring. Evaluate every run on both short-horizon probes and extended rollouts alongside general-domain checks, rather than ranking mixtures on held-out one-step error alone.
  • Baseline variance calibration. Repeat the current baseline mixture to measure its normal score spread before attributing modest shifts to a change in data composition.

Evaluating mixtures under this protocol directly links data allocation to closed-loop behavior, ensuring that additional specialist weight is granted only when it resolves rollout failures rather than polishing single-step loss.

Conclusion

In fixed-budget world-model training, lowering one-step prediction error on specialist footage and improving physical rollout capability do not move in lockstep. Once a small specialist allocation supplies enough exposure to activate or retain a target behavior, pushing that share higher delivers diminishing coverage gains while withdrawing updates from broadly transferable scene data.

Setting the specialist share therefore comes down to three criteria:

  1. Coverage. Early specialist slots yield the steepest increase in event encounter probability; further gains depend on introducing unrepresented failure modes rather than oversampling already-covered transitions.
  2. Retention. Small replay fractions recover most prior-domain performance without impeding adaptation, turning “enough” into a measurable retention target.
  3. Opportunity cost and rollout validation. Reallocating updates incurs a first-order cost governed by ⟨gT, gS − gB⟩, and candidate mixtures must be separated on multi-step rollouts where compounding errors reveal gaps hidden by one-step loss.

The resulting selection rule is straightforward: choose the smallest tested share that reliably meets the target rollout behavior while preserving the capabilities supported by the displaced data. When a smaller and a larger share both preserve object persistence across short occlusions, keep the smaller allocation and spend the remaining update budget on the longer, harder transitions where the model still fails.
