When a small share is enough

The smallest share we tried already improved object persistence.

More footage kept lowering prediction error.

Which result should decide the training mix?

Sicheng Hu

In this essay

Two ways to improve

Roll a red ball behind a screen. For a moment, the right prediction is an empty stretch of tabletop. The interesting frame comes later. Does the same ball come out on the other side?

That distinction changed how I read a set of video-model experiments. The runs began from the same weights and had the same budget of training footage.

In one comparison, the smallest tested allotment of a source rich in physical interactions already improved interaction and object persistence over the starting model. A larger allotment drove down the error on fresh clips from that source. The two rollout scores barely moved beyond their earlier gains.

I would keep a small allocation for that source. It supplies examples of the behavior I care about. To give it more, I would want a new reason, such as a harder interaction the model still gets wrong. Better predictions of familiar footage answer a different question.

The first few clips

Imagine a budget of 36 clips. You can fill some slots with specialist footage and the rest with ordinary scenes. In this toy example, only specialist clips contain the events of interest. A simple crossing appears in one out of four specialist clips on average. A harder crossing involving two objects appears in one out of sixteen.

Give the specialist s slots. If each independent draw contains a particular event with probability r, the chance of missing it every time is (1 − r)s. Its chance of showing up at least once is therefore

C(s)=1−(1−r)s

With eight specialist clips, the common event already has about a nine-in-ten chance of appearing. The next clip raises that chance by only 2.5 percentage points. For the rarer event it adds about 3.7 points. The very same clip slot has different value for two behaviors. These are chances to learn. Whether the model makes use of them is something we can check in a rollout.

This also changes what I would collect. Replaying a familiar clip gives the model another update on it. Adding a recording of an encounter missing from the pool gives the model its first chance to see it. In a run log, I want to see both the number of distinct recordings and the fraction of updates they receive.

A little rehearsal

A small share can also serve a skill the model already has. Ibrahim and colleagues took a language model with 10 billion parameters, trained on the Pile, and continued training it on SlimPajama. They spent the same number of tokens in two runs. In one, five tokens out of every hundred came from the old source. Ibrahim et al., Table 4

Final validation loss in the public replay experiment
Old-source share Old source New source
0% 1.98 2.00
5% 1.79 2.00

The old-source loss had been 1.75 before the switch. A small amount of rehearsal recovered about four fifths of the rise from 1.75 to 1.98, while new-source loss was the same at the reported precision. That gives “enough” a concrete meaning once you specify how much of the old performance you want to retain.

Ye and colleagues turned that reasoning into an experiment. They fitted validation loss as a function of mixture weights. When continuing a language model on Python code and its original text distribution, they used the fitted curves to predict the share that would maintain the original text loss. The retention target gave them a way to choose a point on the curve. Ye et al., Fig. 10

Who gives up a step?

A ratio needs a unit. Suppose a long-video batch fits two clips and a short-video batch fits six. If batches alternate, the long videos get half the optimizer updates and a quarter of the clips. An inventory of files gives a third number. For a trainer that draws one source per update, I count how many updates each source actually got.

Now take one small gradient step. Write gS for the mean gradient from specialist data, gB for the broad data that would give up some of its share, and gT for the gradient of a target loss.

Move a fraction δ of the sampling probability from broad to specialist. To first order, the change in target loss relative to the original update is

ΔLT≈−ηδ⟨gT,gS−gB⟩

Here η is the learning rate. The inner product asks how much of each update points in a direction that reduces the target loss. Even a helpful specialist update can be a poor trade if the displaced update would have helped more. Fan and colleagues use gradient alignment with target domains to learn sampling weights in DoGE. The subtraction above makes the cost of a particular swap explicit. Fan et al., §2

In the video runs, some collections of real scenes also made other real scenes easier to predict. The specialist interaction footage showed little of that transfer to those collections. Taking a step away from a broadly useful source can therefore cost something on a third source whose own share never changed.

Watch the ball come back

In another video comparison, single-step error barely separated two mixtures. Short memory probes also looked much the same. The difference showed up when the model had to carry information across longer sequences.

Once it generates the wrong state, that state becomes the input for its next prediction. Talvitie gives a precise example of how two models with the same one-step errors can behave differently when run forward. Talvitie, §2.1

My next comparison would start both runs from the same weights, keep the step budget fixed, and name the source giving up updates. I’d score the same short and long rollouts in both runs and repeat the current mixture to see how much its scores normally move.

My choice is the smallest tested share that reliably meets the target while the behaviors served by the displaced data still hold up. If both shares bring the ball back after a short crossing, I keep the smaller one. I can spend the remaining steps on the longer crossing where it still disappears.
